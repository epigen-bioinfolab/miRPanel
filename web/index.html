How To Read The Result Table

Every row is one gene, and the columns show what each tool found in it. The tools measure different things in different units, so their numbers cannot be compared with one another. Read across a row to see how far the tools agree, rather than hunting for the biggest number.

Searching more than one miRNA gives you a tab for each one, plus a Merged result tab. The per-miRNA tabs are ordinary single results, so everything in the next tab of this guide applies to them. The merged tab answers a different question: which genes are hit by more than one of your miRNAs?

A gene targeted by several miRNAs with different seeds is a stronger candidate than one found by a single miRNA, because two unrelated miRNAs landing on the same transcript is less likely to be chance.

The two filters

Found by sets how many of your miRNAs must hit a gene before it is listed. With twelve miRNAs from a differential expression list, raising this from 2 to 5 is the quickest way to get from thousands of genes down to a shortlist worth reading.

Site type sets how good each of those hits has to be. 8mer only is the strictest setting, and any site the loosest.

The three buttons

Show genes lists the genes that survive both filters. Download TSV saves that list as a file you can open in Excel or read into R. Run GO and KEGG sends that exact set to pathway analysis, so what you tested is what you filtered.

One row per gene. The columns are grouped by tool, and each group carries a heading saying what its numbers are.

Click a row to open it

The row itself is a summary: the best site each tool found. Opening it shows every individual record, one line each, with the tool, the region, the position on the transcript and the score. The Agreement column on the right is the useful part, because it marks the places where two tools point at overlapping nucleotides. Two tools agreeing on the same stretch of sequence is much stronger evidence than two tools merely naming the same gene.

The toolbar above the table

Search genes looks through the whole result, not just the page you are on. Sort reorders by any tool's score, and the line underneath tells you which criterion is in use. Region drops sites outside the regions you tick. Hide 6mer sites is on by default, because 6mers are weak and there are a great many of them. The count on the left tells you where you are in the result, and Export saves it.

Each tool answers its own question. Here is what each one hands you, and what that number is actually telling you.

Seed analyser

Gives you the kind of seed match, and exactly where it sits.

Which means 8mer is the strongest, then 7mer-m8, then 7mer-A1, then 6mer. Picture a key in a lock: an 8mer is the whole key, a 6mer is two teeth that happen to line up. This is a direct observation about sequence rather than a prediction, which makes it the most reliable column on the page and the weakest evidence on its own.

RNAhybrid

Gives you ΔG, the folding energy of the duplex, in kcal/mol.

Which means the more negative the number, the tighter the predicted pairing. Values usually fall between −15 and −30. Treat them with care: RNAhybrid finds a favourable duplex in roughly nine of every ten genes, so a good energy on its own proves very little. It earns its place when a more selective tool agrees with it.

TargetScan

Gives you context++ and PCT.

Which means context++ predicts how strongly a site will silence its gene, taking into account what surrounds it. More negative is stronger, most values lie between −0.5 and 0, and anything below about −0.3 is worth attention. PCT is the probability that the site has been conserved across species, from 0 to 1, and a value above 0.5 suggests evolution has held on to it, which often means it does something. TargetScan reads 3′UTRs only, so an empty cell may simply mean the site lies elsewhere.

scanMiR

Gives you log Kd, along with the site class and its position.

Which means a model trained on laboratory measurements of how tightly miRNAs bind, so this is the closest thing on the page to a physical quantity. The more negative the value, the tighter the binding. Values usually fall between −1 and −6, and anything below −3 is strong.

miRTarBase

Gives you the number of published experiments for this miRNA and this gene, and how good they were.

Which means this is evidence rather than prediction. Strong support comes from a direct test such as a reporter assay or a western blot, while weak support comes from broader methods such as microarrays. An empty cell means nobody has looked yet, not that the gene is not a target.

Reading a whole row

Because the scores answer different questions in different units, the thing to look for is agreement rather than magnitude.

  • Strongest an 8mer site, a good context++ score and published experimental support behind it.
  • Promising three tools agree on the gene and nobody has tested the pair yet. This is a lead worth following.
  • Weak RNAhybrid alone, because RNAhybrid agrees with almost everything.

Empty cells

A dash means the tool ran and found nothing. N.A. means the tool cannot judge that region, or that organism, at all.

Where to go from here

Tool Agreement draws the overlap between the tools, and clicking any part of it pulls out exactly those genes. Pathway Analysis asks what a set of genes does together. Target Network draws the miRNA and its targets, with thicker links where more tools agree.

Tool 1 of 5 Seed analyser
The question it answers
Does this gene's sequence contain an exact match to the miRNA's seed — the 7 nucleotides (positions 2–8) that do most of the pairing work? A perfect seed match is the classic definition of a binding site, and the rule most target-prediction methods were originally built around.
What it gives you
A site type, and exactly where it sits
Can look at
5′UTR CDS 3′UTR
How to read it

8mer > 7mer‑m8 > 7mer‑A1 > 6mer. An 8mer is the textbook site. A 6mer on its own is weak — most 6mers happen by chance alone.

Tool 2 of 5 RNAhybrid · 2.1.2-8
The question it answers
Could the miRNA and this sequence actually fold together and bind — even where the seed itself isn't a perfect match? Instead of applying a fixed rule, RNAhybrid searches the whole transcript for the single most energetically favourable pairing, closer to how the two strands would really behave inside a cell.
What it gives you
Minimum free energy (ΔG), in kcal/mol
Can look at
Whole transcript
How to read it

More negative means a tighter fold. We only keep hits below −15 kcal/mol; below −25 is genuinely tight. Wobble (G:U) pairs are allowed, so it catches sites the seed analyser refuses.

Tool 3 of 5 TargetScan · v8.0
The question it answers
Taking the sequence around the site into account, how strongly would this gene actually be repressed — and has that exact site been kept in place across species more often than chance alone would explain? A site conserved through evolution is one natural selection has actively protected, which is strong indirect evidence that it's real.
Context++ score
Roughly 0 to −1
Conservation
Conserved sites & PCT
Can look at
3′UTR only
How to read it

More negative context++ = stronger predicted repression (−0.50 is strong, −0.02 is close to nothing). PCT is the probability a site was conserved because a miRNA actually uses it — 0.85 is strong, and no conserved site means no PCT at all. Conservation is counted per gene on its own transcript, so it can't be pinned to one row in the site table.

Tool 4 of 5 miRTarBase · 10.0
The question it answers
Has this miRNA–gene pair actually been tested at the bench, and what did the experiment find? Every other tool here predicts; miRTarBase tells you what has already been measured directly, and how rigorously it was measured.
What it gives you
Experiment counts, and the papers behind them
Can look at
Every gene
How to read it

Strong = a reporter assay, western blot or qPCR — one pair, tested deliberately. Weak = CLIP-seq, microarray, pSILAC — thousands of pairs screened at once. Strong evidence outweighs weak; watch for experiments that reported no effect.

Tool 5 of 5 scanMiR 1.16.0
The question it answers
Combining sequence, structure and real biochemical binding data into a single score, how tightly would this miRNA actually bind here — across the 5′UTR, CDS and 3′UTR all at once? Rather than a rule-based cutoff, this is a model trained directly on RNA binding measurements, the closest thing here to one unified answer.
What it gives you
A binding affinity (log Kd) per site, plus a repression estimate per transcript
Can look at
5′UTR CDS 3′UTR
How to read it

A CNN model trained directly on real biochemical binding data (RNA bind‑n‑seq), not just sequence conservation. More negative log Kd = tighter, more confident binding. Non‑canonical sites are included but flagged low‑confidence, so canonical sites are the safer default view.
